# Horror and tone in OMORI

OMORI's horror works by contrast and accumulation. Critics say so directly: its cute surface hides a grim reality, and
the horror builds from small wrong details rather than set-piece scares (Gamelance, Noisy Pixel, search results and fetched
review text). This file turns that into rules for a mod. Use it with `director.md`.

## What the sources say makes horror work
- **Ask "is it scary?" first.** Frictional Games starts a horror project from that question, not from "is it fun".
  Mechanics are added to serve the emotional goal, and any that hurt the fear are removed (Amnesia's weapon upgrades
  would have "destroy[ed] the horror element"). (Game Developer interview with Thomas Grip, fetched.)
- **Let players scare themselves.** Frictional leaves gaps that the player's imagination fills. Grip's example:
  Slender: The Arrival made ordinary sounds frightening because the player's knowledge did the work. The limit: the
  effect fades, and empty spaces get old.
- **Silence is a tool.** Akira Yamaoka, Silent Hill's sound director, described at GDC 2005 that the team deliberately
  avoided music as a cue for unease, and that empty or formerly lived-in spaces were a reliable tool (Game Developer
  coverage, search result). Silence before a scare raises anticipation; silence after intensity can unsettle more
  than sound (search results).
- **Uncertainty over explanation.** Explaining everything kills the dread on contact (search result on psychological
  horror design). OMORI's writing withholds explanation and reveals its story in fragments (Gamelance).
- **Accumulate.** OMORI's horror builds: a figure at the edge of a scene, an image that flickers for a frame, dialogue
  that lands slightly wrong (Gamelance, fetched).
- **Contrast.** OMORI's bright, saturated Headspace against the muted real world is the engine of its unease
  (Gamelance; Intermittent Mechanism, fetched). Use a deliberate contrast, not a random mix.
- **Pacing critique.** Critics also say long fantasy stretches without stakes and a long mid-game deflate the horror
  (Shetani's Lair; Intermittent Mechanism; Gamelance). Horror needs a release valve: a payoff, a clue, a change.

## Rules for a mod's horror scene
1. The blueprint's GOAL is a feeling, and it is a fear (dread, unease, guilt, wrongness), not a plot point.
2. Each beat adds one wrong detail, not a new scare. Accumulation: the third wrong thing matters more than the first.
3. No jump scares as the main tool. One sudden moment at the peak, if the scene needs it, and it is undone at once.
4. Sound and silence are planned per beat (`audio.md`). A scene with no silence has no room to scare.
5. The player's imagination is trusted. Show the edge of a thing, not all of it.
6. Gore is implied, not shown (the project quality bar; check the content rules of the mod).
7. The vignette and other dark effects are used at the peak and removed straight after. Overuse numbs the scene.
8. The player must still be able to see and move. Darkness that hides the exit is a bug, not horror.

## Tone checks
- **Tone contrast test.** Describe the scene in one sentence as a cute thing with a grim thing inside. If you cannot,
  the contrast is missing or accidental.
- **Repetition test.** If two scenes give the same feeling, one of them has no CHANGES.
- **Explanation test.** Count the lines that explain what the player just saw. Cut half of them.
